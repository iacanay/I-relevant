# Literature review

**Citation priorities after the November 5 search.** For the local-power section, cite Ponomarev (2022, §2.3.3) and Luo and Wang (2018, §5). Both actually compare tests with and without population-redundant inequalities. For the introduction, Chernozhukov, Chetverikov, and Kato (2019), Chesher and Rosen (2020), and Molinari (2020) explicitly discuss the possible precision advantage of retaining them. Ponomarev and Semenova give a worked example of that mechanism. For the formal setup, distinguish our restricted Gaussian mean experiment from unrestricted Gaussian testing and from the representation invariance of Cox and Shi’s conditional chi-square test.

The question of whether retention can change local power already has concrete answers. I would frame our contribution around admissibility under maintained mean restrictions and the size of the gains and losses when the redundant coordinate has sampling variation of its own. The sources checked here leave open how those answers change with the number of core inequalities. They do not establish a priority claim over the entire order-restricted testing literature.

## Ponomarev and Semenova (2025)

**Cite in the introduction.** Kirill Ponomarev and Vira Semenova, *On the Lower Confidence Band for the Optimal Welfare in Policy Learning*, arXiv:2410.07443, September 2025 revision of the October 2024 paper. [Version reviewed](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07443v3).

Remark 2, pp. 12–13, gives the closest example. The share of units with both binary potential outcomes equal to one has a marginal upper bound δ₁ and a tighter covariate-based upper bound δ₂. Since δ₂ ≤ δ₁, imposing θ ≤ δ₁ adds no restriction once θ ≤ δ₂ is imposed. In our sign convention, θ − δ₁ ≤ θ − δ₂ throughout the model. This is exactly the raw population order with one core inequality. The authors’ equation (3.22) gives a reversal once sampling uncertainty is included. For the usual upper confidence level, the looser bound yields the smaller upper endpoint when its precision advantage z₁₋α(σ₂ − σ₁)/√N exceeds δ₁ − δ₂. The comparison is local if the precision advantage is bounded and the population gap is positive. [Remark 2 and equation (3.22)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.07443v3#page=12).

Proposition 1 supplies the developed policy example. Along a sequence approaching a welfare tie, treating everyone is slightly suboptimal, but its welfare can be estimated more precisely than welfare under the oracle optimal policy. The gain in their lower-endpoint criterion is of order N⁻¹ᐟ². Section 2.3 defines that criterion as a large-sample analogue of the difference in expected endpoints. Section 5.1 separately combines policy inequalities by inverting a jointly calibrated max test. The policy that is optimal can vary with the data-generating process, which makes Remark 2 the cleaner example of a fixed redundant inequality. [Sections 2.3, 3 and 5.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.07443v3).

**Implication for our contribution.** Chesher and Rosen (2020, pp. 104–105) raised the precision argument. Ponomarev and Semenova give it concrete content. I would credit them for the possibility of a first-order inferential gain from a looser population bound. Our question is what happens when the researcher keeps both inequalities in the test. We ask whether another level-α test can dominate that joint test and how much local power is gained or lost relative to the core-only test. The ranking of two separate confidence endpoints does not settle either comparison.

The existing project draft already cites the 2024 version and Remark 2 on printed p. 2. Its observation that a critical-value effect can arise even at equal marginal variances is worth preserving. The citation decision here is to retain and position the reference.

## Which inequalities suffice for identification?

**Galichon and Henry (2011), *Set Identification in Models with Multiple Equilibria*, Review of Economic Studies.** In §2.3 of the authors’ version, Definition 7 and Proposition 1 explain when checking event probabilities against capacity bounds on a smaller collection suffices for sharpness. Under the ordering and connectedness conditions of Theorem 4, initial and terminal segments suffice. With I ordered outcomes this leaves at most 2I − 2 nontrivial bounds. Cite for the computational motivation. [Authors’ version](https://arxiv.org/html/2102.12249).

**Chesher and Rosen (2017, 2020).** Theorem 3 of *Generalized Instrumental Variable Models* (Econometrica) gives sufficient conditions for reducing the collection of test sets in the space of unobservables. Their *Handbook of Econometrics* chapter develops this construction in §5.2, pp. 40–43. For our introduction, the passage to cite is §9.1, pp. 104–105. They explicitly ask whether a moment excluded from a core determining class might be estimated more precisely and help inference on a functional of interest. [2017 article](https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA12223) · [2020 chapter](https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.hoe.2019.11.001) · [Local chapter](../references/Chesher-Rosen.pdf).

**Pakes and Porter (2024), *Moment Inequalities for Multinomial Choice with Fixed Effects*, Quantitative Economics.** Theorem 2 gives conditional sharpness. In §4, (14)–(15) show how restrictions (10) imply the two-cycle restriction (13). The converse can fail. This is a model-based example in which one can append a restriction without changing identification. To apply our Gaussian results, we would also need to check nonsingularity for the chosen sample moments. [Article](https://doi.org/10.3982/QE1776).

**Luo and Wang (2017), *Core Determining Class and Inequality Selection*, American Economic Review.** Cite for the exact (smallest) core determining class and graph-based conditions for finding it. The power comparison is reported in a separate working paper indexed below. [Article](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.p20171041).

## What does deletion do to inference?

**Luo, Ponomarev, and Wang, *Selecting Inequalities for Sharp Identification in Models with Set-Valued Predictions* (accepted at the Review of Economic Studies, author-posted version dated April 13, 2026).** Theorem 1 characterizes a smallest core determining class in their finite-outcome setting. Section 4.3.1, p. 25, nn. 13–14, is the passage to cite on inference. A collection can suffice for the population and plug-in identified sets while omitted inequalities still matter for power at particular alternatives. They cite Canay and Shaikh’s Example 4.1 below. Their §6 comparisons also allow informal selection to lose identifying information. Our keep/drop comparison fixes the population null. [Working paper](https://wang-hai.net/file/selecting_inequalities_full.pdf).

**Bontemps and Kumar (2020), *A Geometric Approach to Inference in Set-Identified Entry Games*, Journal of Econometrics.** See Proposition 7 and §5.2, Table VI, in the authors’ manuscript. In one four-player design, geometric selection reduces the number of inequalities evaluated from 92 to 18, with the same reported confidence-region size. Cite for the computational saving. Ranking the tests by power at a given alternative would require rejection probabilities at that alternative. [Authors’ manuscript](https://www.cemmap.ac.uk/wp-content/legacy/uploads/Bontemps-Kumar-JofEc-december2019.pdf).

**Ponomarev (2022), *Essays in Econometrics*, UCLA dissertation, ch. 2, §2.3.3, pp. 87–91, Fig. 2.10, p. 92. Cite in Section 4.** This is the closest comparison I found. Equations (2.5)–(2.8) set up a studentized max test of Artstein inequalities. The author compares two core inequalities with the same two plus an implied inequality, using each maximum’s own least-favorable Gaussian critical value. The display on p. 90 shows that the third standardized statistic equals (σ₁X₁ + σ₂X₂)/σ₀, where the first two statistics are X₁ and X₂ including their local means. Consequently, the three-coordinate covariance is singular. The third statistic adds a rejection direction without supplying an independent third source of sampling variation. Figure 2.10 plots short-minus-long local power for six designs. The author reports gains from inclusion when both core drifts are positive in those designs. This is numerical evidence for the ranking. The limiting power formulas themselves are derived. [UCLA dissertation, full text](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1kz2n299).

Our working experiment allows a separate third innovation and restricts its mean. Moreover, in Ponomarev’s raw units the third violation is the **sum** of two core violations. Our two-core model bounds it above by their **maximum**. The sum can exceed that maximum when both cores are violated. The models thus share the logical implication under the null while differing in their alternatives as well as their covariance. Cite the dissertation for the power comparison and the later three-author paper above for its core-class characterization.

**Luo and Wang (2018), *Identifying and Computing the Exact Core-determining Class* (working paper). Cite in Section 4.** Full-text check from Ivan’s copy. In Example 5’s two-type entry game, Table 1 (printed p. 22) reduces the 126 nontrivial Artstein inequalities for seven latent events and twelve observed outcomes to 11 defining the same feasible set Q. Sections 5.1–5.2 (pp. 24–25) compare S₁, the sum of squared positive studentized violations, and S₃, their maximum. Taking the square preserves the max-test rejection rule when the cutoff is transformed with it. Each collection gets its own resampling critical value. They resample outcomes, center the bootstrap moments, include the moment-selection adjustment, regularize the covariance (ε = .01), and use the 1 − α + η quantile (η = 10⁻⁶). [Full paper, §§5.1–5.2](../references/luo-wang.pdf#page=25) · [Repository record](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/sis_research/4337/).

Section 5.3 (pp. 25–27) selects a boundary point v by maximizing (1,2,…,7)′v over Q. The candidates lie on the single outward ray v + td/√n, with d = (−1,−1,−1,1,1,1,0)/√6 and t from 0 to 3. The authors use n = 1,000, nominal α = .05, 500 bootstrap resamples and 1,000 Monte Carlo replications per t. Table 3 (p. 27) and Figure 6 (p. 52) report simulated rejection frequencies. At t = 1.5 deletion raises S₁ rejection from 0.1% to 64.2%, and S₃ rejection from 68.1% to 72.9%. At t = 0 the full/core rates are 0.0%/0.1% for S₁ and 0.5%/1.4% for S₃. The boundary conservatism differs, so the raw power gaps also reflect calibration. This is evidence for a gain from deletion along one ray when 115 inequalities are removed together. It leaves open the cost of dropping one inequality elsewhere and uniform dominance over alternatives. Section 5 describes candidate-indexed cutoffs, whereas §5.3’s shorthand displays a cutoff indexed by v. The exact implementation of that index would require the code. [Table 3](../references/luo-wang.pdf#page=28).

**Chernozhukov, Chetverikov, and Kato (2019), *Inference on Causal and Structural Parameters Using Many Moment Inequalities*, Review of Economic Studies.** The introduction of the authors’ manuscript, p. 2, explicitly gives a reason for retaining more inequalities than sharp identification requires: an implied moment can be estimated precisely. The observation already appears on p. 2 of their 2014 working paper. Cite this alongside the Handbook chapters. The many-moment critical-value theory addresses a different selection problem from deletion based on a known population implication. [Article](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdy065) · [Authors’ manuscript, p. 2](https://denischetverikov.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/moment_ineq_v27.pdf#page=2) · [2014 version](https://www.cemmap.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/CWP5214.pdf).

**Molinari (2020), *Microeconometrics with Partial Identification*, Handbook of Econometrics.** The discussion on printed p. 110 of the arXiv manuscript explicitly distinguishes computational savings from the possible statistical value of a precisely estimated redundant inequality. Molinari identifies choosing inequalities for a specified finite-sample objective as open. In the same passage, the 26-inequality sharp characterization and the 18-inequality outer region illustrate why two different comparisons can easily be conflated. [Author’s version, p. 110](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.11751#page=111).

**Cox and Shi (2023), *Simple Adaptive Size-Exact Testing for Full-Vector and Subvector Inference in Moment Inequality Models*, Review of Economic Studies. Cite when defining redundancy.** The remark after Algorithm 1 (§3.1, pp. 14–15 of the authors’ manuscript) states that their conditional chi-square test is invariant to adding an implied linear inequality to the description of a fixed polyhedron. The projected point and the rank of its active restrictions are geometric features of that polyhedron. Our additional sample moment can vary conditional on the core observations even when its population inequality is implied. Their invariance establishes an exact benchmark for alternative descriptions of the same feasible region. The remark also suggests gains from reducing other tests. Non-invariance alone gives no power ordering for our experiment. [Authors’ paper, Algorithm 1 and remark](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~xshi/research/CoxShiTest.pdf#page=14).

## Gaussian testing benchmarks

**Canay and Shaikh (2017), *Practical and Theoretical Advances in Inference for Partially Identified Models* (published chapter).** Example 4.1, pp. 281–283, studies one-sided testing with an unknown, unrestricted Gaussian mean vector and known covariance, and discusses admissibility. Our maintained mean ordering removes alternatives at which only a redundant inequality is violated. This changes the set of alternatives over which a competitor must improve power. [Chapter](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108227223.009).

**Romano, Shaikh, and Wolf (2014), *A Practical Two-Step Method for Testing Moment Inequalities*, Econometrica.** Ivan’s reference for how to introduce the testing problem and benchmark power. Read pp. 1979–1982 of the published article, then Supplement §S.1 for the Gaussian analysis. With known covariance, they test the nonpositive orthant against its full complement. Section S.1.1 gives a power upper bound for level-α tests and §S.1.2 develops their two-step procedure. Our mean restriction narrows that complement. [Published article](https://home.uchicago.edu/amshaikh/webfiles/twostep.pdf) · [Supplement, §S.1](https://home.uchicago.edu/~amshaikh/webfiles/twostep_appendix.pdf).

## Further sources screened on November 5

- **Identification examples.** Beresteanu, Molchanov, and Molinari (2011), supplemental four-player game, reduce the sharp system from 512 candidate inequalities to 26 by checking which equilibrium outcomes can occur together. Chesher, Rosen, and Smolinski (2013), §3.3, Theorem 2, give a parameter-dependent core class for an instrumental-variable discrete-choice model. Mbakop (2023 working paper), §2.4.1, eliminates redundant linear inequalities in discrete-choice models. These are options for an economic example of implication. [Beresteanu–Molchanov–Molinari, article and supplement](https://molinari.economics.cornell.edu/docs/ecta8680.pdf) · [Chesher–Rosen–Smolinski](https://doi.org/10.3982/QE240) · [Mbakop](https://wpsites.ucalgary.ca/eric-mbakop/wp-content/uploads/sites/32/2023/05/Identification_in_Some_Discrete_Choice_Models_A_computational_Approach.pdf).
- **Earlier use of core classes in testing.** Galichon and Henry’s *A Test of Non-Identifying Restrictions and Confidence Regions for Partially Identified Parameters*, Remark 1, uses a reduced family of events to make a specification test feasible. This gives an early bridge from a population characterization to a test. The remark supplies no power ranking across the two families. Kline, Pakes, and Tamer’s industrial-organization Handbook chapter, §6.1, explains the computational motivation for removing inequalities implied by others. [Galichon–Henry, Remark 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.04151#page=8) · [Kline–Pakes–Tamer](https://tamer.scholars.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum7756/files/tamer/files/partial_identification_in_industrial_organization2_copy_-2.pdf#page=96).
- **Related power comparisons.** Chen and Lee (2019) preserve the identified set in discrete choice by conditioning on indices and compare the resulting test with a test conditioning on the full covariate vector. Their Table 3 in the 2015 working paper reports gains for the index method in almost all simulated configurations. Here the change affects the dimension of nonparametric estimation. Bugni, Gao, Obradović, and Velez, §3.2, hold the interval bounds fixed and vary the precision of the estimators used in four confidence intervals. The power ranking depends on the interval construction. Both are useful comparisons if we discuss precision beyond our specific keep/drop problem. [Chen–Lee article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2018.12.024) · [Chen–Lee Table 3](https://www.cemmap.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/CWP2615.pdf) · [Bugni et al., §3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.20386#S3.SS2).
- **Other inferential objectives.** Moon and Schorfheide (2009) study empirical likelihood when equalities already point identify the parameter. The published abstract establishes an asymptotic MSE gain from the overidentifying inequalities and uniform validity for inference. I would cite the MSE result. The old draft’s stronger statement about uniform improvement in test power needs a specific proposition before use. Kaido and Molinari’s information-based procedure uses a core class to describe feasible distributions in a score construction. Kaido and Zhang’s robust likelihood-ratio test instead optimizes worst-case power over distributions compatible with an incomplete model. Neither provides the full/core max-test comparison considered here. [Moon–Schorfheide](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2009.05.003) · [Kaido–Molinari, n. 23 and supplement lemma cited there](https://people.bu.edu/hkaido/pdf/KaidoMolinari25.pdf) · [Kaido–Zhang](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.04610).
- **Classical Gaussian precedents.** Cohen and Sackrowitz (1992) establish inadmissibility for specified tests with zero or equal means under the null, ordered alternatives and a common unknown variance. Andrews’ *Similar-on-the-Boundary Tests for Moment Inequalities Exist, But Have Poor Power*, Theorem 1, studies what similarity on the boundary costs in multivariate one-sided testing. These are precedents for the dependence of power conclusions on the null, alternative and test class. Neither result identifies an effect of deleting a known redundant coordinate in our mean space. [Cohen–Sackrowitz](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1992.10476270) · [Andrews, Theorem 1](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d1815.pdf).

**Ponomarev’s research page.** The posted Luo–Ponomarev–Wang manuscript is the April 13 version discussed above. Cite its §4.3.1 for the distinction between sharp identification and inference, and the dissertation for Figure 2.10. The page lists the Ponomarev–Semenova welfare paper as an R&R at *Quantitative Economics*. It also lists *Efficient Estimation of Intersection Bounds* with Bulat Gafarov as work in progress, without a manuscript link. Its overlap with our project remains unknown. [Author’s research page](https://kirillponomarev.com/research/) · [Posted Luo–Ponomarev–Wang paper, p. 25](https://kponomarev.github.io/files_on_website/selecting_inequalities_full.pdf#page=25).
